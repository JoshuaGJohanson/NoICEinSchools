Granite School District · Board Meeting Prep

Preparing for the Granite School Board Meeting: Updated Talking Points

Research-backed talking points, supporting data, and framing for advocates speaking at the Granite School Board about student safety during immigration enforcement activity.

Use this page alongside Working with the Granite School District Board. That page covers how to register, what to expect, and how to coordinate speakers. This page covers what to say.

Goals

Every family feels safe sending their children to a Granite school

When families fear that immigration enforcement could appear at school, attendance drops and learning suffers. A Stanford study of five California districts found that intensified immigration enforcement coincided with a 22 percent increase in student absences in early 2025,[1] with absences spiking about 30 percent in pre‑K and 27 percent in grades K‑5.[1]

Every teacher knows how to handle questions and what to do in an emergency

Legal experts note that many school officials are anxious about potential ICE visits and "uncertain about what to do."[2] Schools that lack clear procedures leave teachers to improvise, increasing stress and the risk of mistakes.

Granite's current policies

Granite already has meaningful protections in place. These are the foundation the proposed solutions build on — not a starting point from scratch.

Student access & records

  • Granite does not ask for or collect immigration status — all children are entitled to a free public education regardless of status
  • Student records are protected by FERPA and are not shared with immigration officials
  • Birth certificates used for enrollment verification do not include citizenship status

Law enforcement & safety

  • The Granite Police Department does not inquire about immigration status or cooperate with ICE unless there is an immediate safety emergency
  • If a parent or guardian is detained during school hours, the district's crisis and emergency response team will support affected students
  • Families are encouraged to update emergency contact information through the Parent Portal or by contacting their child's school

Non-discrimination

  • Granite prohibits discrimination and harassment based on nationality, race, ethnicity, and other protected classes
  • Incidents can be reported to a school principal, district administrator, or the Student Access & Opportunity Department

Family support

  • The Student Access & Opportunity Department provides resources and language support for families with limited English proficiency or new arrivals
  • Contact: 385-646-4205
The gap is not values — it is operational detail. Granite's policies establish the right commitments. What is missing is a documented, school-level procedure for what front office staff do when ICE agents arrive at or near campus.

Current situation

Students and families are scared

Attendance and enrollment are down. A working paper from Stanford's Institute for Economic Policy Research found that local ICE partnerships reduced Hispanic student enrollment by nearly 10 percent within two years, displacing more than 300,000 Hispanic students.[3] A separate Stanford study using daily attendance data documented a 22 percent increase in absences when immigration raids intensified,[1] with the largest effects on younger students.[1]

Many Granite families live in mixed‑status households. Granite serves communities in West Valley City, Kearns, and Taylorsville with significant immigrant and mixed-status populations. Nationally, more than 16.7 million people share a home with at least one undocumented family member, including 6.1 million U.S.-citizen children under 18.[4] When enforcement activity increases, Granite families in these communities face real pressure to keep children home to avoid scrutiny.

Students are anxious and distracted. A 2025 national survey of over 600 high‑school principals found that:

  • 70.4% reported heightened student worry about the well‑being of immigrant family members
  • 63.8% said students from immigrant families missed school due to policies or rhetoric
  • 35.6% reported immigration‑related bullying[5]

Chronic stress and fear undermine learning and lead to lower scores.

Key numbers

22% increase in student absences during enforcement activity[1]
10% decline in Hispanic enrollment under local ICE partnerships[3]
6.1M U.S.-citizen children living with an undocumented family member[4]
63.8% of principals report immigrant students missing school[5]

Educators lack clear guidance

Granite has a foundation — but no operational protocol for ICE encounters. The district's published guidance addresses FERPA protections, GPD's non-involvement with immigration officials, and a crisis response team for situations where a parent is detained. What is missing is a school-level procedure for what front office staff do when ICE agents arrive at or near campus: who to contact, how to verify a warrant, what to say, and how to manage dismissal. Legal experts note that school officials are anxious about ICE visits and uncertain what to do.[2] Nationally, the UCLA principal survey found that only 77.6 percent of principals had created any plan, and fewer than 45 percent had provided professional development for staff[5] — a gap Granite's 90+ campuses are not immune to.

Granite's published FAQ does not cover what happens when ICE is at the door. In January 2025, the federal government revoked the "sensitive locations" memorandum that previously discouraged ICE from entering schools. According to the Immigrant Legal Resource Center, ICE may now enter public areas of a school (such as parking lots and lobbies) but still needs permission or a judicial warrant to enter private spaces like classrooms.[6] Granite's FAQ confirms that GPD does not interact with immigration officials except in safety emergencies — but it does not tell a front office staff member what to do, what to ask for, or who to call when an ICE agent walks in.

ICE activity near schools is real. Reporting from The New Yorker describes how a suburban Minnesota district was "swarmed with ICE agents," with parents sheltering in place or being detained and teachers acting as chauffeurs and bodyguards to get children to class.[7] The article recounts teachers physically positioning themselves between agents and students.[7] Even when agents remain off campus, their presence in nearby parking lots creates fear and chaos.

Proposed solution — three practical asks

We are asking Granite School District to adopt three measures already used by leading districts across the country.

1. Clear plans for ICE activity near schools

  • Designate a single ICE response lead (principal or trained administrator) at each school — all staff direct agents to this person
  • Define protocols for when ICE is observed near campus, including delayed-release procedures until the area is safe
  • Require multiple emergency contacts and pre-authorized alternate pickups; prepare short‑term care plans if parents are detained[6]
  • Coordinate community‑led escort programs — walking buses, carpools, or bike buses — during arrival and dismissal

2. Comprehensive Know‑Your‑Rights training for staff, students, and families

  • Train staff to distinguish administrative vs. judicial warrants and understand what information they can legally share[6]
  • Work with community organizations to offer Know‑Your‑Rights workshops; display signage and red cards explaining that students have the right to remain silent and to refuse entry without a judicial warrant[6]
  • Core rights students should know:[6]
    • You have the right to remain silent — you do not have to answer questions about your immigration status, where you were born, or how you entered the country
    • You do not have to open a door or allow entry without a judicial warrant signed by a judge
    • You have the right to speak to a lawyer before answering questions or signing anything
    • If stopped, ask "Am I free to go?" — if yes, calmly walk away
    • Do not physically resist, even if you believe your rights are being violated
  • Train students to memorize an emergency phone number and to avoid signing documents or answering questions without a lawyer — so they can protect themselves if they encounter ICE off campus[6]
  • Encourage parents and caregivers to teach children their rights and how to assert them
  • Teachers who share Know-Your-Rights information with students are protected — providing factual legal information about constitutional rights falls within academic freedom; the district should explicitly affirm that educators will not face discipline for teaching students their legal rights
  • Partner with legal aid groups and the Student Access & Opportunity Department to provide accessible, multilingual training and materials[12]

3. Clear, proactive communication with families

  • Publish district policies explaining protections and procedures in multiple languages; send real‑time alerts via phone, text, email, or school apps when ICE activity is reported near campus[12]
  • Establish protocols for notifying parents and caregivers promptly; never share student information with ICE[6]
  • Encourage families to keep emergency contacts up to date and to talk with children about who is authorized to pick them up and what to do if plans unexpectedly change
  • Include guidance in communication plans on how families can locate detained relatives and connect with legal assistance
  • Consistent, transparent communication reduces rumors, builds trust, and ensures families feel safe sending their children to class

Implementation and benefits

Modeled on emergency plans

Schools already have protocols for fires, lockdowns, and natural disasters. Plans for immigration enforcement are a logical extension of existing safety planning.

Improves academic outcomes

Research on California's safe‑zone policies found that they positively impacted standardized test scores and high‑school graduation rates, particularly among vulnerable and immigrant students.[8]

Supports teachers and boosts morale

Clear guidance reduces stress and ensures that teachers are not left to improvise in high‑pressure situations. Professional development helps educators understand their legal responsibilities and empowers them to reassure students.

Restores trust and attendance

When families know that schools will protect their children and respect legal process, they are more likely to keep children enrolled. Evidence shows that fear of enforcement dramatically increases absences[1] — addressing that fear can reverse the trend.

Prepares for increased ICE activity

With a proposed ICE detention center near Granite District, enforcement activity may intensify. Being proactive avoids the chaos and trauma experienced in communities like Columbia Heights.[7]

Talking points for speakers

Each point can stand on its own as a 2-minute public comment. Assign different points to different speakers so the board hears a full picture.

Why now — the situation in Utah

  1. ICE activity is real and increasing in Utah communities — including the neighborhoods Granite students live in.

    ICE made between 3,040 and 4,164 arrests in Utah in 2025 alone — more than double, and in some analyses nearly quadruple, the number in 2024.[9][10] Arrests now frequently occur outside jails ("street arrests"), rising to over 100 in a single month in Utah,[11] and can take place "almost anywhere" in the community — including the neighborhoods, routes, and public spaces Granite students use every day. ICE does not report activity specifically near schools, but the geographic risk is real.

  2. Real communities are already facing these issues.

    In Columbia Heights, Minnesota, ICE agents patrolled near schools and detained students, forcing teachers and parents to act as bodyguards and chauffeurs.[7] Proactive planning can prevent similar chaos in Granite District.

  3. High impact in Granite.

    Granite serves more than 60,000 students — Utah's largest district — with a majority non-white student population. Hispanic/Latino students make up the largest non-white group, with significant concentrations in West Valley City, Kearns, Taylorsville, and Midvale. The district also serves one of Utah's largest Pacific Islander populations and growing refugee communities from East Africa and Southeast Asia. A policy here protects more students than any other single district action in Utah.

Building on Granite's foundation

  1. Granite's existing policies already protect students.

    The district does not collect immigration status, limits cooperation with ICE through GPD, and protects records under FERPA. These proposals build on that foundation — they complete it.

  2. This proposal is proactive, not political.

    The ILRC explains that after revocation of the sensitive‑locations memo, ICE can enter public spaces like parking lots but still requires a judicial warrant for private areas.[6] Planning for this reality upholds the law while ensuring student safety.

  3. We are not asking for anything beyond the law.

    Schools cannot deny admission based on immigration status, as affirmed by Plyler v. Doe.[2] Our proposals simply call for following legal process and protecting students' rights.

The case for clear plans

  1. This is consistent with other safety plans.

    Schools already prepare for fires, lockdowns, and health emergencies. Planning for immigration enforcement is the same idea — it creates order instead of ad‑hoc reactions.

  2. Clear plans ensure consistent responses across all 90+ campuses.

    A documented protocol means every school responds the same way — no improvising, no inconsistency depending on which administrator is on duty.

  3. Planning prevents chaos — Granite teachers are already making ad hoc plans on their own.

    Without a district protocol, individual teachers are deciding for themselves how to respond to ICE encounters — improvising under pressure with no guidance. In other districts, that improvisation has meant teachers physically positioning themselves between agents and students[7] and acting as bodyguards and chauffeurs to get children safely to class.[7] Granite teachers should not be left in that position. A clear district plan protects staff as much as it protects students.

The case for Know-Your-Rights training

  1. Know‑Your‑Rights training empowers staff and students.

    Staff need to know the difference between an administrative and judicial warrant. Students need to know their rights. Partnering with legal aid groups and the Student Access & Opportunity Department makes this training practical and accessible.

  2. Teachers need support.

    Without guidance, teachers are left to make their own decisions. Legal experts note that school officials are anxious and unsure how to respond;[2] providing protocols and training improves morale and protects staff from legal missteps.

The case for clear communication

  1. Transparent communication builds trust and keeps children in school.

    Families need to hear directly from the district — in their language — what protections are in place and what the district will do. Fear of enforcement dramatically increases absences;[1] clear communication directly addresses that fear.

  2. Trust is essential.

    Families need to know that schools will protect their children. When trust erodes, attendance and enrollment plummet. Transparent policies and proactive communication build confidence.

  3. Community escort programs help students arrive safely during heightened enforcement.

    Walking buses, carpools, and bike buses give families a safer option when ICE activity is reported near schools. These programs work best when the district coordinates and communicates proactively.

Why it works — the evidence

  1. Student safety drives learning.

    Chronic stress and fear undermine learning. Principals report that immigration‑related fear causes students to miss school (63.8%) and experience bullying (35.6%).[5] Ensuring families feel safe keeps kids in class, improves focus, and raises test scores.[8]

  2. Evidence‑based benefits.

    Safe‑zone policies in California improved academic outcomes and graduation rates.[8] Adopting similar policies in Granite will help our students succeed.

Notes for presenters

Use personal stories to illustrate statistics

Examples from families and teachers humanize the data. Lead with a specific story, then support it with the research.

Reference major districts that have acted

Los Angeles, New York City, Chicago, Denver, and San Francisco have already adopted similar warrant policies and training programs. This is mainstream practice — not a fringe ask.

Keep the ask modest and specific

Clear procedures, know‑your‑rights training, and transparent communication. These align with existing legal requirements and best practices and are already working elsewhere.

Reminder: you have 2–3 minutes. Choose one talking point, support it with one statistic, and end with the specific ask. Speakers who try to cover everything are less persuasive than speakers who make one clear, memorable argument.
